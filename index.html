A historical inquiry into Jesus of Nazareth

The Galilean

What the stones, coins, manuscripts, and hostile witnesses can tell us about Jesus of Nazareth, followed through a single day: from the cold light of the museum to the lake, the city at dusk, the dark, and the morning after.

PrologueCaesarea Maritima26–36 CE

Before you begin

This is not a sermon, and it is not an attempt to debunk anything. It is a guided walk through the physical and written evidence for a man who lived in Galilee two thousand years ago. Nearly everything you will meet here can be visited: a stone in a museum in Jerusalem, a coin in a drawer in London, a scrap of papyrus in Manchester, a page in a library in Florence. Where a famous piece of evidence turns out to be weaker than its reputation, I say so. Where an obscure one turns out to be stronger, I say that too.

You do not need to know anything about ancient history, or about me, to follow it. Every technical term is explained the first time it appears. Every claim is sourced at the end.

Come with whatever you believe or doubt. The stones do not ask.

Prologue · Caesarea Maritima · 26–36 CE

A Stone with a Name on It

In the summer of 1961, an Italian archaeological team was clearing the ruins of the Roman theatre at Caesarea, on the coast of what is now Israel. In one of the staircases they found a limestone block that had been used, sixteen centuries earlier, as an ordinary step. Someone in the fourth century had needed building material and had grabbed an old monument. They set it face down, which is the only reason its letters survived. When the block was turned over, it read: PONTIVS PILATVS.

Pontius Pilate is the Roman official who, according to all four Gospels, ordered the execution of Jesus. Before 1961, he was known only from books: the Gospels, two Jewish writers of the first century, and a few lines in Tacitus. Some sceptics had wondered whether the Gospel writers had the details right. The stone settled it. A man with this name and this title governed this province in the years the Gospels describe, and he cared enough about his standing with Rome to put up a building in the emperor’s honour and carve his own name on it.

That is all the stone says. It does not mention a trial. But it does something important: it puts a Gospel character into the physical record, in the right place, in the right decade, with the right job. This inquiry begins here because it is the simplest kind of evidence there is, and because it sets the rule for everything that follows. Before we ask what Jesus meant, we should find out what can actually be touched.

A word I will use often: receipt

Historians of the ancient world work with very little. For most people who lived in the first century we have nothing at all. For most of the famous ones we have a name, a rough date, and stories written down long afterward. So it helps to have a word for the kind of evidence that does not depend on anyone’s faith or wishes. I call it a receipt. A receipt is a stone with a name carved in it, a coin, a line in an official record, or an admission from someone who would rather not have made it. A receipt proves that something happened whether or not you like the person it happened to.

For a poor man from a village in Galilee we should expect no receipts. Instead we have a governor’s stone, a high priest’s bone box, coins that passed through the markets he walked through, a mocking cartoon scratched on a wall in Rome, and four accounts of his life written while people who had known him were still alive. Those four accounts disagree about details, and their authors made no effort to smooth the disagreements away. That is worth noticing. When four people describe the same event and agree on everything, someone has been coordinating. When they agree on the main facts and differ on the small ones, you are looking at what real memory looks like.

Alongside the receipts, I will apply three tests that historians use on any ancient story, religious or not. The tests are simple, and it helps to state them plainly, because everything that follows leans on them.

Three tests

1. Is it embarrassing? Would the people who preserved this story have preferred that it were not true? People do not invent details that undermine their own cause. If a story includes something awkward for the storytellers, that awkward detail is probably real.

2. Is it independently reported? Do sources that did not copy from each other agree on it? One report can be a rumour. Two that did not know each other are hard to dismiss.

3. Do the enemies admit it? Did people who hated the movement concede the fact anyway? An opponent has every reason to deny, so an opponent’s admission is the strongest kind of confirmation.

A detail that passes any one of these tests is probably history. A detail that passes all three is about as solid as ancient history gets. There is more of that solid ground under this story than most people, believers or not, have been told.

I · Jerusalem, the southern tombs · first century

The Weight of Dirt

In November 1990, a road crew working in a park south of Jerusalem’s Old City broke through the roof of a burial cave from the first century. Inside were twelve stone boxes of a kind called ossuaries. Jewish families in Jerusalem at that time buried their dead in a rock-cut tomb, waited about a year for the flesh to decay, and then gathered the bones into a small limestone box, usually with the person’s name scratched on the side. One of these twelve boxes was far finer than the rest, carved with elaborate rosettes. On its end, scratched twice in a rough hand, was a name: Yehosef bar Qayafa. Joseph, son of Caiaphas.

Caiaphas is the high priest who, in the Gospels, presides over the night-time hearing that sends Jesus to Pilate. He held the office from 18 to 36 CE, an unusually long run, which suggests he was good at working with Roman governors. He and Pilate arrived in Judaea within a year of each other and were removed within a year of each other. The two men who share a page in the Gospel account of one Passover also share a decade in the administrative record of the province.

One more detail from the tomb. In another of the boxes, inside a woman’s skull, lay a bronze coin minted in 42 or 43 CE. Placing a coin in the mouth of the dead was a pagan custom, a fare for the ferryman of the underworld. It turned up in the family tomb of the high priest. Real people are never as tidy as the official version of them. The coin does not prove anything about Jesus. It reminds us that the people in this story were people.

A second box needs more caution, and I will give it exactly as much as it deserves. In 2002, a collector in Tel Aviv produced an ossuary with the inscription Ya’akov bar Yosef akhui d’Yeshua: James, son of Joseph, brother of Jesus. The box itself is certainly ancient. The first half of the inscription is very probably ancient. The last two words, the ones that matter, have been fought over for more than twenty years. The Israel Antiquities Authority declared them a modern forgery. An Israeli court, after a seven-year trial, found that the forgery could not be proved, while declining to declare the inscription genuine. That is where things stand: unproved either way.

I mention it for one reason. Of the roughly two thousand ossuaries known from the Jerusalem area, only one other names the dead person’s brother, and it does so because the brother was the more famous of the two. James, Joseph, and Jesus were all common names, as common as John and Michael today. To identify a dead James by his brother Jesus, you would have to mean that Jesus, the one people had heard of. If the words are genuine, a first-century family was pointing at a first-century man. If they are forged, the forger understood the first century very well. Either way, the box teaches a habit worth keeping: hold uncertain evidence lightly, and build on nothing you cannot stand on.

Here is what can be stood on. The Gospels are not set in a vague once-upon-a-time. They are set under a named emperor, a named governor, a named high priest, and a named ruler of Galilee, in named towns. The emperor Tiberius is on every silver coin of the period. Pilate is on the stone at Caesarea. Caiaphas is in a box in the Israel Museum. Herod Antipas, the ruler of Galilee who executed John the Baptist, struck coins in the lakeside city he built. John the Baptist himself appears in Josephus, a Jewish historian writing at the end of the century. So does James the brother of Jesus, stoned to death in 62 CE on the orders of a high priest. The setting is real, and the cast checks out. Fiction does not work this way. Myths do not leave receipts.

II · Rome · 64 to c. 200 CE

What the Enemies Admitted

Rome kept records too, and it kept them with contempt. Tacitus was a Roman senator and the most respected historian of his generation. Around the year 116 he wrote an account of the great fire that destroyed much of Rome in 64 CE, and of how the emperor Nero, to deflect blame from himself, blamed a group called Christians and had them tortured to death. Tacitus pauses to explain to his readers who these people were. The only surviving copy of this part of his work is a manuscript made by monks in the eleventh century, now in a library in Florence.

“Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus, and a most mischievous superstition, thus checked for the moment, again broke out not only in Judaea, the first source of the evil, but even in Rome.”

Tacitus, Annals 15.44, translated by Church and Brodribb

This is what historians call a hostile witness, and it is the third of the three tests in its purest form. Tacitus despised Christians. He calls their belief a disease. He has no reason at all to help their story along. Yet he confirms its skeleton: a man, executed under the emperor Tiberius, by the governor Pontius Pilate, in Judaea, with a movement that should have died with him and did not. Contempt has no motive to flatter. That is exactly why contempt makes good evidence.

A short walk uphill from the Forum, the contempt becomes a picture. In 1857, excavators on the Palatine Hill opened a room in a building where the imperial household trained its slave boys. The plaster walls were covered in graffiti. One drawing shows a man on a cross. The man has the head of a donkey. To the left, a smaller figure raises one hand toward him in worship. Underneath, in clumsy Greek with a spelling mistake: “Alexamenos worships his god.”

Think about what the joke needed in order to work. By around the year 200, a slave in the emperor’s own household could be mocked, by other slaves, for worshipping a man who had been crucified, and everyone in the room got the punchline without explanation. The donkey’s head was a standard slur against Jews and Christians. The cross needed no gloss at all. Crucifixion was the most degrading death the Roman world had. It was reserved for slaves, rebels, and bandits. Roman citizens were exempt from it by law. Cicero, the great orator, told a jury that the very word “cross” should be kept far from a Roman citizen’s body, thoughts, eyes, and ears. The Jewish historian Josephus called it “the most wretched of deaths.”

And the Christians put it at the centre of everything. They did not hide it or soften it into a noble martyrdom. Paul, writing in the fifties, says the cross is “a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles,” and that he will preach nothing else. Apply the first test. No one designing a religion to appeal to the Roman world would choose a crucified founder. You would carry that fact only if it were true and you had no choice, and if something had happened afterward that made the shame impossible to hide and unnecessary to try.

One more thing the graffito and Tacitus have in common. Neither of them thinks Rome did anything unusual. Rome did not kill Jesus out of some special wickedness. It killed him because it was orderly: a governor keeping the peace at a festival, a high priest protecting the Temple, a legal procedure that worked exactly as designed. The system had no category for the man in front of it, so it used the category it had. That is a more unsettling thought than villainy, and it is the one the evidence supports.

The oldest surviving picture of the crucifixion is an insult. Someone in the next room answered it: Alexamenos is faithful.

III · Nazareth and the Sea of Galilee · c. 4 BCE – 30 CE

The Country of the Lake

Now leave the museums and go north, to the small hill country where the story actually happened. Almost everything Jesus is remembered for saying was said outdoors, in villages around a lake, to farmers and fishermen whom no one in Rome or Jerusalem expected to matter.

Nazareth was nowhere. The Gospels do not hide this; they preserve a sneer from the period, “Can anything good come out of Nazareth?”, which is the kind of detail the first test tells us to trust, because no one flattering their founder would have kept it. But nowhere in Galilee was ever far from somewhere. Six kilometres over the ridge, Herod Antipas, the local ruler, was rebuilding the city of Sepphoris into a Greek-speaking capital, with colonnaded streets and mosaic floors. The construction went on through Jesus’s whole youth. The Gospels call his father a tekton, the Greek word usually translated “carpenter,” which really means a builder who worked in wood and stone for wages. A builder’s son from Nazareth would have known the road to Sepphoris the way a farm boy knows the road to the county seat, and would have seen there what a city does to a village, and what an empire does to a city.

He did not stay in Nazareth. By about the year 28 he had moved to Capernaum, a fishing town of perhaps fifteen hundred people on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee. Capernaum sat on the border between two small territories, which is why it had a customs post, which is why a tax collector turns up among his first followers. The lake itself explains a great deal. It is twenty-one kilometres long and thirteen wide, and it lies two hundred metres below sea level, walled in by hills that funnel sudden winds down onto the water. It has always been fished at night, by men in small boats who mend their nets in the morning. When Jesus’s parables reach for a net, a boat, a catch of fish, or a squall that comes out of nowhere, he is not reaching for anything. He is describing the view from the shore.

Capernaum has been excavated since Franciscan monks bought the site in 1894. Under the white limestone synagogue that visitors photograph, which dates from the fourth or fifth century, the excavators found walls of black basalt: the floor and foundations of the first-century synagogue, the one the Gospels say a Roman centurion paid for and Jesus taught in. Thirty metres away is a plain courtyard house of the same period that later generations treated strangely. Its main room was plastered and replastered, scratched with Christian prayers in three languages, then enclosed in a church, then in a larger octagonal church built on top. Something about that one room mattered to people within a generation or two of its owner. The Gospels say Simon Peter, the leading disciple, lived in Capernaum with his mother-in-law. The archaeology shows a fisherman’s house that became a shrine long before Christians had any power to build shrines.

Six kilometres down the shore, in 2009, a religious order began building a guesthouse at the town of Migdal. Israeli law requires an archaeological check before construction, so the Antiquities Authority dug first. What they found stopped the project. Under the car park was the ancient town of Magdala, the home of Mary Magdalene: streets, a market, ritual baths, a harbour, and a synagogue of the first century with its stone benches still in place, its walls painted, its floor a mosaic. In the middle of the room stood a carved limestone block about the size of a footstool. On its face, in relief, was a seven-branched lampstand, the menorah, the great golden lamp that stood inside the Temple in Jerusalem.

This matters because of an old argument. For much of the last century, scholars pictured Galilee as a backwater only loosely attached to Jerusalem, its Judaism thin, its villages open to every passing idea. The Magdala Stone ends that argument. In a fishing town of a few thousand people, someone carved the holiest furniture of the Temple into a synagogue stone, with a precision that suggests personal knowledge. The Galilee Jesus walked was a devout Jewish country that faced south, toward the city, the festivals, and the house of God. When he came into conflict with the Temple, it was a quarrel inside the family. That is why it hurt.

Picture a Sabbath morning in the basalt room at Capernaum. Fishermen’s hands, the smell of the lake on their clothes, children restless on the stone benches. The reader unrolls the scroll of Isaiah. A man from Nazareth, in his early thirties, already known for saying things no one had said in that room before, stands up to speak. What he says is remembered the way lake people remember things: by picture. The kingdom of God is like a net. Like a seed. Like a woman who has lost a coin and lights a lamp to find it. Like a man who owes more than he can pay and is let off. In everything of his that was written down, there is hardly a single idea that a child on that bench could not have pictured.

On one of these hillsides, Matthew’s Gospel says, Jesus sat down and taught. The opening of that sermon is known as the Beatitudes, from the Latin word for “blessed.” It is probably the most quoted passage in Western literature, and I have come to think it is the least carefully read. Here it is in full.

Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.

Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.

Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.

Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy.

Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.

Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God.

Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness’ sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

Blessed are you when others revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account. Rejoice and be glad, for your reward is great in heaven, for so they persecuted the prophets who were before you.

Matthew 5:3–12

Notice what these sentences are not. They are not advice. They do not tell the poor to work harder, the grieving to cheer up, or the meek to stand up for themselves. They are announcements. They tell people who had never been told they had won anything that they have already won, and they are spoken by a man who was about to become poor, grieving, meek, hungry, and persecuted himself, and to be shown no mercy at all. Keep these nine sentences in mind. We will come back to this hillside at the end, because the whole story does.

IV · Jerusalem · Passover, c. 30 CE

Two Coins

Every spring, Jewish pilgrims from all over the country travelled to Jerusalem for Passover, the festival that remembers the escape from slavery in Egypt. From Galilee the usual route ran south down the Jordan valley to Jericho, then up a thousand metres through desert hills to arrive with the Temple in view. Passover was a celebration of freedom from a foreign empire, held in a city occupied by a foreign empire, and every coin in every pilgrim’s purse was part of that tension. Two coins matter to this story. They are often confused with each other, and it is worth getting them straight.

The first coin is bronze, and it is the one Jesus would have handled as everyday change. It tells us something about Pilate. Given a free hand to design a small coin for a religiously sensitive province, he chose to put pagan ritual objects on it. Philo, a Jewish philosopher in Egypt who was alive at the time, describes Pilate as inflexible, stubborn, and merciless. Josephus records him marching military banners bearing the emperor’s image into Jerusalem by night, and later seizing money from the Temple treasury to build an aqueduct and having the protesters beaten. The coin matches the descriptions.

The second coin is silver, and it is the one that appears in a famous Gospel scene. Jesus’s opponents ask him a question designed to trap him: “Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?” If he says yes, the crowds who hoped he would stand up to Rome will hear a collaborator. If he says no, Pilate’s soldiers will have a charge of sedition before sunset. He asks to see the coin the tax is paid in. Someone produces a denarius, which in the Temple courts is a small embarrassment in itself. Then he asks whose face and whose inscription it carries. The face is the emperor’s, wearing a laurel wreath like a god. The inscription calls him the son of a god.

“Whose likeness and inscription is this?” They said to him, “Caesar’s.” Jesus said to them, “Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s.” And they marvelled at him.

Mark 12:16–17

This answer is often read today as a neat rule about keeping religion and politics separate. The people in the courtyard heard something sharper. Everyone there knew the first page of their scriptures, where human beings are made “in the image of God.” The coin carried Caesar’s image, so the coin could go back to Caesar. But every person standing there carried God’s image, including the men holding the silver. In one sentence Jesus handed Caesar a coin and claimed everything else, every human being in the courtyard, for God. It escaped the trap. It also drew a line that the men who ran the Temple could not fail to notice.

The Temple that spring was the largest religious complex in the Roman world, its platform the size of thirty football fields, still under construction after fifty years. It was the one institution in Judaea that Rome could not simply replace, so Rome managed it through the high priesthood, and the high priesthood managed Rome. Into its outer court, during the busiest week of the year, Jesus walked and overturned the tables of the money-changers, the men who exchanged pilgrims’ Roman coins for the special coinage the Temple tax required. Historians disagree about exactly what he meant by this. They mostly agree that the men who ran the Temple took it as a threat to the whole arrangement. From that day, Mark’s Gospel says, they looked for a way to have him killed. Of all the events of that week, this is the one historians are most confident about.

V · The texts · read against their own interest

Reading the Gospels Against Themselves

Before the story reaches its last night, it is worth pausing to show how historians read the Gospels, because the last night is where that reading gets tested. The four Gospels are the main sources for Jesus’s life, and they were written by believers, for believers, between roughly forty and seventy years after his death. That does not make them worthless as history. It means they have to be read the way a good detective reads any interested witness: looking hardest at the things the witness would rather not have said.

Start with the first test, embarrassment. Jesus began his public life by being baptised in the Jordan by John, a preacher who offered baptism as a sign of repentance for sin. That was awkward for the early church, which believed Jesus was sinless and greater than John. Watch what happens to the story over time. Mark, the earliest Gospel, states the baptism flatly. Matthew, written later, adds a scene in which John protests that it should be the other way around. Luke mentions the baptism only in passing. John’s Gospel, the latest, never quite says it happened at all. Four writers become steadily more uncomfortable with a fact none of them felt free to leave out. That is not how legends grow. It is how real memories get managed.

The Gospels keep many such details. Jesus’s own family thought he had gone mad; Mark says so directly. His home town rejected him, and Mark says that there “he could do no mighty work.” Peter, the disciple the church would later build on, denied three times, to a servant girl, that he even knew Jesus. All four Gospels keep that scene. Women were the first to find the tomb empty, in a culture where a woman’s testimony did not count in court, and the men did not believe them; every account keeps that too. And from the cross itself the Gospels preserve a cry that no theologian would ever have chosen to put in the mouth of God’s chosen one: “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”

The three tests, applied

Embarrassing details the writers kept anyway: the baptism by a supposed inferior, the family who thought him mad, the chief disciple who denied him, the women as first witnesses, the cry from the cross.
Facts reported by sources that did not copy each other: Mark, the traditions behind John’s Gospel, Paul’s letters, and Josephus stand at different distances from the events and had no common editor. They agree on the baptiser, the brother, the execution under Pilate, and the followers who refused to disperse.
Facts the enemies conceded: Tacitus says Pilate executed him. The Talmud, the collection of rabbinic tradition, says he was hanged on the eve of Passover. The earliest Jewish argument against the resurrection, recorded in Matthew, assumes the tomb was empty and offers a reason.

Now consider the oldest physical piece of the New Testament in existence. It is the size of a credit card. In 1920 a British scholar bought a batch of papyrus scraps in Egypt for a library in Manchester. In 1934 a young researcher sorting the batch recognised a few dozen Greek letters. The scrap is written on both sides, which means it came from a book with pages rather than a scroll. The handwriting style dates it to the second century, within a few decades of the Gospel’s composition. The text is from the eighteenth chapter of John’s Gospel. Of all the passages that could have survived on the oldest fragment we have, these are the ones that did.

The oldest surviving scrap of Christian scripture is a page from a trial. On it, a Roman governor asks a Galilean prisoner what truth is, and then walks out of the room without waiting for an answer, because he has a restless crowd to deal with. I do not think the accident of what survived means anything. I notice that I keep coming back to it anyway.

The oldest page we have is a governor asking what truth is, and leaving the room before the answer.

VI · Jerusalem · Thursday night

Thursday Night

The oldest written account of Jesus’s last evening is not in a Gospel. It is in a letter that Paul sent to the church in Corinth, in Greece, around the year 55, only about twenty-five years after the events. Paul was scolding the Corinthians for turning their shared meal into a free-for-all, and to correct them he quoted words he had himself been taught, in the fixed rhythm of something recited from memory: “the Lord Jesus, on the night when he was betrayed, took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it, and said, This is my body.” Within a generation, in a Greek port city hundreds of miles away, the words of that supper were already a formula that everyone knew. Whatever else about Thursday is uncertain, the meal is not.

Jesus and his followers ate in the city, in an upstairs room lent by someone whose name was not recorded. Afterward they went out through a gate and down into the Kidron valley, the deep ravine on the east side of Jerusalem. The valley was a cemetery then, and it still is. Cut into its far slope were the monumental tombs of wealthy Jerusalem families, including a tall cone-topped monument that visitors today call Absalom’s Tomb. It was new then. Jesus had recently mocked the pious for “building the tombs of the prophets and decorating the monuments of the righteous.” He walked past those very monuments, in the dark, on the way to a garden on the hill across the valley.

What happened in the garden was reported by the people who slept through it. That is worth noticing. The disciples’ own account of their last night with their teacher is that he asked them to stay awake with him and they could not, and that when the arresting party arrived with torches and clubs, one of them swung a sword, cut off a servant’s ear, and then all of them ran away. Nobody polishes a founding story like that. They kept it because it was what happened, and because, by the time it was written down, the men who had run were the ones doing the telling.

Jesus was taken back across the valley and up into the Upper City, the wealthy western hill where the priestly families lived. Excavations there in the 1970s uncovered their houses: mosaic floors, painted walls, stone furniture, private ritual baths, and in one burnt room a stone weight still bearing the name of a priestly family, exactly where it fell when the Romans destroyed the city in 70 CE. To one of these houses, Caiaphas’s, Jesus was brought at night for what the Gospels call a trial and most historians call a hearing: the high priest and his advisers working out, before the governor woke, what charge they could bring that Rome would act on. Blasphemy would not do; Rome did not care about Jewish theology. A claim to be a king would. Pilate did not need a doctrine. He needed a word that meant rebellion.

Down in the courtyard, John’s Gospel says, the servants had lit a charcoal fire because the night was cold, and Peter stood by it warming his hands. A servant girl looked at him twice. The Greek word for that kind of fire appears exactly twice in the entire New Testament. The second time is on a beach, and we will come to it. For now it is enough to say that a fisherman from Capernaum, asked three times whether he knew the prisoner, said three times that he did not, and that somewhere in the city, in the last dark before dawn, a rooster crowed.

What happened in the garden was reported by the people who slept through it.

VII · Golgotha · Friday, nine in the morning to three in the afternoon

The Cross

At first light they took him to Pilate. During festivals the governor stayed in the old palace of Herod the Great on the western hill, a fortress of gardens and audience halls that Josephus describes in the tones of someone who had been inside. Most historians now think the exchange preserved on the Manchester papyrus happened there, not at the Antonia fortress that the traditional pilgrim route remembers. Pilate’s question was the only one that mattered to Rome: “Are you the King of the Jews?” The answers the Gospels record are evasive. Pilate had a crowd, a festival, and an anxious priesthood on his hands. He had the prisoner flogged, which was standard procedure before a crucifixion, and sent him out to die, which was routine, and ordered the charge written on a board to hang above his head.

The board read, according to John, “Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews,” in Aramaic, Latin, and Greek, so that every pilgrim could read it. The priests objected to the wording and the governor refused to change it. Historians have long noticed that the sign has the exact tone of Roman sarcasm. It was Pilate’s joke, aimed less at the dying man than at the priests who had brought him: this is what your king looks like. A church inventing the scene would have written something nobler. The sign is too cruel and too clever to be anything other than what a bored provincial official actually ordered painted that morning.

What crucifixion did to a body is known from exactly one piece of physical evidence, found by accident in 1968 when bulldozers cutting a road for a housing development north of Jerusalem broke into a first-century family tomb. Among the bone boxes was one inscribed with the name Yehohanan. Inside were the bones of a man in his mid-twenties, and through his right heel bone, still, an iron nail eleven and a half centimetres long.

Yehohanan corrects the paintings. There was no standard method of crucifixion, only a general idea, hanging a person up to die slowly, and the soldiers’ improvisation within it: sometimes ropes, sometimes nails, sometimes a small seat, the body positioned so that breathing itself became the exhausting labour that eventually killed. The Gospel writers do not describe any of this, and people have wondered why. Yehohanan answers that too. Everyone in Jerusalem had seen a crucifixion. You did not describe one to a first-century reader any more than you would describe a car to a reader today.

Yehohanan also answers a modern objection. Some scholars have argued that a man crucified by Rome would simply have been left for the dogs, and that the Gospel story of a proper burial is a pious invention. But Yehohanan was crucified by Rome, in the same city, in the same generation, and he was taken down, laid in his family’s tomb, and a year later gathered carefully into a box with his name on it. Jewish law required burial the same day, and Josephus confirms that the Jews of his time took even crucified men down before sunset. The Gospels say that a member of the council that had condemned Jesus, Joseph of Arimathea, asked Pilate for the body and was given it. A member of the very council: a detail that embarrasses the council and complicates the story, kept anyway. All three tests point the same way.

The place of execution was outside the city wall, beside a road, in an abandoned stone quarry that had been filled with soil and turned into gardens, with tombs cut into its rock faces. Its name was Golgotha, “the skull.” Mark gives the hours in the Roman way. At the third hour, nine in the morning, they crucified him. At the sixth hour, noon, darkness came over the land. At the ninth hour, three in the afternoon, he cried out in a loud voice. The words are preserved in his own language, Aramaic, because no one dared translate them into something gentler.

Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani? which means, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”

Mark 15:34

Then he died. Before sundown, when the Sabbath began and all work had to stop, the body was taken down, wrapped in cloth, and carried a short distance to a new tomb cut into the quarry wall. A stone was rolled across the entrance. The women who had followed him from Galilee, Mark says, watched where he was laid. Remember that. Everything that happens on Sunday depends on their knowing which door.

VIII · A quarry outside the wall · Saturday

The Empty Tomb

Saturday is the day about which the sources say nothing. The city kept the Sabbath. The disciples were in hiding. The women waited for daylight, because the work of mourning could not begin until the Sabbath ended. History, too, has nothing for Saturday except a door with a stone across it. I want to look carefully at that door before anyone opens it.

Tombs of this period are well understood, because nearly a thousand of them survive around Jerusalem. A low doorway is cut into a rock face and closed with a stone. Inside is a chamber you can stand in, with benches along the walls where a body was laid while the flesh decayed, and narrow shafts cut deeper into the rock where bones, or the boxes holding them, were stored afterward. Round rolling stones like the one in the photograph were rare and expensive; most tombs used a square plug. The Gospels describe a new tomb, cut from rock, closed by a stone that had to be rolled, near the place of execution, and owned by a rich man. Every one of those details is the kind archaeology can check, and every one checks out.

Whether the tomb inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre is the tomb is a separate question, and I will answer it as exactly as the evidence allows. The site was a limestone quarry from the eighth century BCE until about the first century BCE. Then it was filled with soil and used, as worked-out quarries often were, for gardens and graves; first-century tombs of the standard type are cut into the rock a few metres from the shrine, and one of them can still be crawled into. In the year 30 the spot lay just outside the city’s wall, beside the road to the coast. About ten years later a new wall was built that brought it inside the city. This matters. If the location was invented, the invention had to happen within a decade of the events, by people pointing at a place then inside the walls and claiming it had once been outside. In 135 CE the emperor Hadrian flattened the area and built a temple of Venus on precisely this spot. In 326 the emperor Constantine’s engineers demolished the temple, dug through Hadrian’s fill, and found, in the words of a contemporary, against every expectation, a tomb.

None of this proves that the bench under the marble is the bench Jesus was laid on. It proves that the place is the right kind of place; that the first Christians of Jerusalem located the tomb there within living memory; that Rome thought the memory worth burying under a temple; and that the shrine standing today is continuous with the one built when the memory was three hundred years old. For ancient history, that is a great deal. It is not everything, and I will not pretend it is.

A famous stone that is not in this inventory

You may have read that a marble slab in Paris, the “Nazareth Inscription,” proves that Rome responded to the empty tomb. It carries an imperial decree threatening death for anyone who removes a body from a grave, and it was bought in 1878 by a collector who noted only that it had been “sent from Nazareth.” The unusually harsh penalty invited the idea that the emperor was reacting to reports from Galilee. In 2020, a team of researchers analysed the chemistry of the marble and traced it to a quarry on the Greek island of Kos, where the tomb of a local ruler had been notoriously vandalised a generation before Jesus. The decree is almost certainly about Kos. It stays off the shelf. An inventory that cannot leave things out is not an inventory. It is a shrine.

What the door itself cannot tell us, the opponents can. The oldest surviving argument against the resurrection is preserved in Matthew’s Gospel, which quotes it in order to answer it: “His disciples came by night and stole him away.” A century later, a Christian writer named Justin reports that Jewish leaders were still spreading the same explanation. Around the year 200, another writer, Tertullian, mocks a version in which a gardener moved the body to protect his lettuces from the crowds. Notice what all these explanations have in common. They all explain an empty tomb. Not one ancient opponent, in any text that survives, says the obvious thing: the body is still there, go and look. In a city that has given us the bones of Caiaphas and the heel of Yehohanan, that silence carries weight.

It also has a limit, and here I have reached it. History can walk you to the door of the tomb. It can date the mortar, measure the stone, and tell you who was standing there and what they said afterward. It cannot go inside. Neither could the women. They came to a door in the half-dark and found it open, and everything that follows, for them and for anyone since, begins not with an argument but with a decision about what to do next.

Every ancient argument against the resurrection is an explanation of an empty tomb.

IX · The garden · Sunday, before sunrise

The First Day

And very early on the first day of the week, when the sun had risen, they went to the tomb.

Mark 16:2

Mark, the earliest and plainest of the Gospels, sets the scene in one sentence and then does something no propagandist would do. Eight verses later, his book ends with the women running from the tomb in terror, having told no one: “for they were afraid.” That is where the oldest manuscripts stop. Later copyists, unable to bear such an ending, added others. The original is a door left open, and it is either the clumsiest ending in ancient literature or the most honest. Three women, one of them from the fishing town with the carved lampstand, went to a tomb with spices to finish a burial that Friday had cut short. On the walk, they worried about the stone.

Now turn from the Gospels to the oldest Christian document of all. Around the year 55, Paul reminded the Corinthians of what he had been taught when he himself became a Christian, some twenty years earlier. He quotes a short, formal statement of belief, a creed, with the balanced phrases of something learned by heart, and says he received it and passed it on unchanged. Scholars who agree on almost nothing else agree that this is the earliest Christian text we have, and that it was already fixed when Paul learned it in the mid-thirties, within a few years of the crucifixion, from people who had been in Jerusalem that week.

For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. Then he appeared to more than five hundred brothers at one time, most of whom are still alive, though some have fallen asleep. Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles. Last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me.

1 Corinthians 15:3–8

“Most of whom are still alive.” That is not the voice of legend. It is the voice of a man naming witnesses in an argument and telling his readers they can go and ask them. Cephas is Peter, under his Aramaic name, which shows that the creed was composed in Aramaic before it was translated into Greek, in Jerusalem, among the people who called him that. James is the brother who had once thought Jesus was out of his mind. Paul himself had hunted Christians for a living. This is not a list of believers. It is a list of people who had every reason not to be.

Here the historian meets a real puzzle, and it is not the one people usually name. Whether a dead man can live again is not a question the tools of history can answer. They were not built for it, and it is no shame to a spade that it cannot weigh a soul. The question history can ask is narrower and harder to escape. Movements built around a messiah die with him. Judas the Galilean, who led a revolt in 6 CE, was killed and his followers scattered. Theudas was beheaded and forgotten. The man called the Egyptian, Simon bar Giora, Bar Kokhba: Rome killed each of them, and each movement dissolved within months, as such movements do. This one did the opposite. Within weeks, its leaders were preaching publicly in the city that had executed their teacher, to crowds that included the men who had arranged it. Within about three years, its most ferocious persecutor, Paul, had joined it on the road to Damascus, and he spent the rest of his life being beaten for it, five times with thirty-nine lashes by his own count, until Rome executed him too. Within thirty years, the brother who had thought Jesus mad was leading the church in Jerusalem, and was stoned to death for it on the orders of a high priest; Josephus, who was in the city at the time, records the killing without sympathy. Within eighty years, a Roman governor in what is now Turkey was writing to the emperor for advice about people who gathered before dawn to sing hymns “to Christ as to a god.”

Something happened. That sentence is as far as the historical method can go, and it is further than most people think. A historian is not obliged to say what happened in the garden. A historian is obliged not to pretend that nothing did. Frightened men who had run away became public witnesses in the most dangerous city on earth for them to do it in, stayed witnesses under torture, and in no record we have ever took it back. Their explanation of themselves was simple and they never varied it: they had seen him. I am not going to tell you what they saw. I am going to tell you that the evidence for their transformation is as solid as the evidence for the theatre at Caesarea, and that every alternative explanation of that transformation I have read in twenty years of reading them has required more faith than theirs.

X · The road to Emmaus, and back · Sunday

The Road Out of the City

Luke’s Gospel keeps a story the others do not, and it is the one I have come to think this whole inquiry was walking toward. On the Sunday afternoon, two of Jesus’s followers left Jerusalem. They were not famous ones; one is called Cleopas and the other is never named. They were walking to a village called Emmaus, seven miles away, which is to say they were going home, or at least going somewhere that was not the city where everything had failed. A stranger fell in beside them on the road and asked what they were talking about. They told him. Their summary of the week is the most honest sentence in the New Testament.

But we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel.

Luke 24:21

“We had hoped.” Past tense. They were walking away from a grave, and the stranger walked with them, and, Luke says, starting with Moses and the prophets he explained the scriptures to them as they went, and they did not recognise him. That is the second honest thing in the story. They had followed him for months and did not know his face. When they reached the village they urged the stranger to stay, because it was getting dark, and at the table he took the bread, blessed it, broke it, and handed it to them. In that gesture, not in the argument on the road, they knew him. And then he was gone.

They said to each other, “Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?” And they rose that same hour and returned to Jerusalem.

Luke 24:32–33

Seven miles back, in the dark, on the road they had walked that afternoon in the other direction. That is the shape of the story, and once you have seen it, you see it throughout the record. It is the shape of the very first thing Jesus is reported to have said in public. Mark gives it: “The kingdom of God is at hand; repent.” The Greek word is metanoeite, and English has turned it into a word about feeling sorry. The Hebrew idea underneath it, the word every prophet before him had used, is shuv: turn around, come back. “Return to me, and I will return to you,” the prophet Malachi had written. The first thing Jesus asked of anyone was not to move forward. It was to turn.

Now go back to the hillside, as I said we would, and read the Beatitudes again.

Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.

Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.

Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied.

Blessed are the merciful, for they shall receive mercy.

Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.

Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God.

Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness’ sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.

Matthew 5:3–10

Each sentence has two halves, and the halves do different jobs. The first half names people: the poor, the grieving, the meek, those who hunger for justice, the merciful, the clean-hearted, the peacemakers, the persecuted. It points in a direction, toward the people at the bottom, and it is the direction most of the modern world learned from him without remembering where it learned it. The hospital and the orphanage, the belief that the weak have a claim on the strong and that the stranger is owed something, the discomfort you feel when someone mocks the poor: all of that was taught on this hillside. People who have not been inside a church in thirty years still feel that discomfort. It is right that they do. It is his.

But each sentence has a second half, and a direction is not the same thing as a destination. Read the second halves on their own and notice what they are. “Theirs is the kingdom”: a people in exile given their country back. “They shall be comforted”: the prophet Isaiah’s words to a nation coming home from Babylon. “They shall inherit the earth”: a line from the Psalms about the humble receiving the land that was promised to them and lost. “Satisfied”: the hunger of the wilderness ended. “See God”: face to face, as in the garden at the beginning of the Bible, before that was lost too. “Called sons of God”: the name a father uses for a child who has come back through the door. Every one of the promises is a homecoming. Not one of them is a frontier. Jesus never told anyone to advance. He told them the kingdom had come close, and to turn around.

There is a way of reading these nine sentences that hears only the first halves and builds a programme for improving the world on them. It is not wrong. It is half. There is a way of reading them that hears only the second halves and builds a private comfort on them, and that is half too. The sentences are whole only when the direction and the homecoming are held together, in one hand, and the one hand that ever held them together was the hand that broke the bread at Emmaus. A drive to make things better that has forgotten where home is becomes mere motion. A longing for home that has forgotten the poor becomes mere nostalgia. He offered neither one alone. He offered the road out of the city and the road back, walked on the same evening, by the same two people, changed.

He told the story of the return himself, and Luke kept that too. A younger son asks his father for his inheritance early, which in that world was close to wishing the father dead, takes it to a far country, spends it all, and ends up feeding pigs and envying them. “But when he came to himself,” Luke says, in the plainest possible words for what happened, he decided to go home and ask to work as a hired hand. Then comes the line that Rembrandt spent the last years of his life painting.

And he arose and came to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him.

Luke 15:20

The father runs. Apply the first test to that detail. In the villages of Galilee, a man of standing did not run. It was undignified; it meant hitching up your robe like a child or a servant. The people on the hillside would have laughed at the picture, and then stopped laughing. Nobody inventing a God would make him run. And the story ends, like Mark’s Gospel, with a door: the elder son standing outside the party, his father come out to plead with him, and no verse to tell us whether he went in. Jesus did not finish his stories. He left them open at the exact point where the listener had to decide.

And the whole story, the entire Gospel record, ends by turning around. “He is going before you to Galilee,” the young man at the tomb tells the women in Mark; “there you will see him.” Not Jerusalem. Not Rome. Back to the lake. The last chapter of John’s Gospel puts the fishermen in a boat at night on the water they grew up on, catching nothing, with a stranger on the shore at daybreak. When they come in, there is a charcoal fire. It is the second and last time that word appears in the New Testament. Peter, who had warmed his hands at the first one while denying three times that he knew Jesus, is asked three times at the second one whether he loves him, and three times says yes, and is given his work back. The story does not end at a grave. It ends at breakfast, on a beach, at sunrise, with the man who had failed worst being fed first.

A drive to make things better that has forgotten where home is becomes mere motion. A longing for home that has forgotten the poor becomes mere nostalgia. He offered the road out of the city and the road back.

XI · The inventory, totalled

What We Can Say for Certain

Here is the inventory, totalled. What follows is not what believers hold or what sceptics grant. It is what the working consensus of professional historians of the period, Jewish, Christian, and neither, has arrived at after two hundred years of trying hard to disagree. Where the consensus is thinner, I say so. The last item is the one that is usually labelled “disputed.” Nobody actually disputes it. It is simply not the kind of question that historical method can settle, and I have labelled it accordingly.

  1. A Jew called Yeshua lived in Galilee in the early first centuryVirtual unanimity

    Tacitus and Josephus, neither of them friendly, establish it without any help from the Gospels.

  2. He was born around 6–4 BCE and grew up in NazarethStrong consensus

    The stories of his birth are contested. The Nazareth origin is too embarrassing to have been invented and too widely attested to drop.

  3. He was baptised by John the BaptistStrong consensus

    Four writers visibly uncomfortable with a fact none of them felt free to omit. Josephus independently confirms John.

  4. He taught in parables, gathered disciples, and was known as a healer and exorcistStrong consensus

    Even hostile rabbinic tradition credits him with wonders, and blames sorcery.

  5. He caused a disturbance in the Temple courts at PassoverMajority view

    Reported independently by Mark and John. The likeliest immediate cause of his arrest. A few scholars dissent.

  6. He was crucified on the orders of Pontius Pilate, around 30 CEVirtual unanimity

    Tacitus, Josephus, Paul, all four Gospels, and the Talmud. The stone at Caesarea supplies the governor.

  7. He was buried in a rock-cut tomb by Joseph of ArimatheaMajority view

    A minority argues that crucified men were left unburied. Yehohanan of Giv’at ha-Mivtar was buried, and re-buried, with care.

  8. Women followers found the tomb empty on the SundayMajority view

    Reported by all four Gospels, and assumed by the earliest hostile explanation, which alleges theft rather than denying the emptiness.

  9. His followers had experiences they were convinced were appearances of him aliveVirtual unanimity

    That they had such experiences is not in doubt; scholars who agree on nothing else agree on this. The creed in 1 Corinthians 15 dates to within about five years of the events.

  10. The movement survived his death and spread across the Roman worldHistorical fact

    Pliny, Tacitus, the Alexamenos graffito, the catacombs, Dura-Europos.

  11. What emptied the tombBeyond the method

    Not disputed. Not that kind of question. The historian walks you to the door and stops, and says so.

Notice what this consensus is made of. Details that embarrassed the people who preserved them. Facts reported by sources that had never read each other. Admissions from witnesses who wished the whole thing would go away. Tacitus, with access to the records of the Roman Senate, confirms the execution under Pilate. Josephus, who had no patience for messiahs, mentions the man, his brother, and the followers who were still around in the nineties. The Talmud, hostile throughout, remembers a Yeshua hanged on the eve of Passover for sorcery and leading Israel astray. Pliny reports the hymns before dawn. A Roman schoolboy draws the donkey on the cross. Even the enemies agreed on the skeleton of the story. The argument was never about whether these things happened. It was about what they meant.

So here is history’s verdict, and it is not a shrug. A Jewish teacher from a village no one had heard of lived, taught with an authority that alarmed his own family and enraged his enemies, died on a Roman cross as a public joke, and started something that has not stopped. It is customary to end an inquiry like this by saying that whatever happened in the tomb, call it resurrection or call it the power of an idea, either way we are left with a mystery. I decline the custom. There is no “either way” at a door. The women did not stand outside it weighing hypotheses. They went in, and came out, and ran.

“He comes to us as One unknown, without a name, as of old, by the lakeside, He came to those men who knew Him not. He speaks to us the same words: ‘Follow thou me!’ and sets us to the tasks which He has to fulfil for our time. He commands. And to those who obey Him, whether they be wise or simple, He will reveal Himself in the toils, the conflicts, the sufferings which they shall pass through in His fellowship, and, as an ineffable mystery, they shall learn in their own experience Who He is.”

Albert Schweitzer, The Quest of the Historical Jesus, 1910

Epilogue

Morning

I walk early, before the day gets busy, in a park near my home in Texas that is not Galilee and does not pretend to be. The sun comes up over live oaks instead of the hills beyond the lake. Nobody is fishing. The only stone underfoot is limestone laid down by a sea that dried up before there were hills to see it from. It is enough. The sun does not argue with anyone. It rises, and everything that looked grey turns out to have had a colour the whole time.

This inquiry began with what could be touched, and it has never stopped touching things. The letters on the stone at Caesarea are exactly what they were in 1961, and in the year they were cut. The lake is a lake. The heel bone is in a museum case in Jerusalem with a nail through it. What changed, over the year I spent gathering all this, was not the evidence. It was the hour of the day I found myself reading it in. In the cold light of a museum, a stone is a receipt. Carry it with you through a whole day, from the museum to the lake to the city to the dark and out the other side, and it becomes a door. Same stone.

That is all I will say about that. It is the kind of thing a person says once, at the end of a book, and then goes outside.

Read the nine sentences again. Then read them once more. Then, if you can, get up before the light some morning, go somewhere quiet, leave your phone in your pocket, and watch what the women watched. Every road in this story went out from the city in the dark and came back the same night, walked by people who had hoped, in the past tense, and then simply hoped. The stones are where they always were. So is the road back.

The sun had risen.
